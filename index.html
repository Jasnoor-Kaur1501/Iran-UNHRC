01 — Agenda Overview
What This Agenda Is Actually About
Read this first — your foundation for everything else in committee.
The Core Problem

Climate change is displacing millions of people — floods, droughts, rising seas, desertification — yet international law does not formally recognize "climate refugees." The 1951 Refugee Convention only covers people fleeing persecution. Climate-displaced persons fall into a legal grey zone: no formal protection, no rights, no status. The UNHRC is tasked with fixing this gap.

Key Legal Gaps
  • 1951 Refugee Convention — excludes climate victims entirely
  • Paris Agreement — mentions displacement but has no enforcement
  • Sendai Framework — disaster risk only, not legal refugee status
  • Guiding Principles on IDPs — not legally binding
  • Statelessness — sinking island nations (Maldives, Tuvalu) will create stateless people with no legal protection
Key Stakeholders Globally
  • UNHCR — mandate does not cover climate refugees
  • IPCC — provides climate science underpinning displacement claims
  • IOM — tracks migration data but lacks enforcement
  • Pacific Island Forum — front-line nations demanding legal recognition
  • Loss & Damage Fund (COP28, 2023) — new but underfunded
  • Global Compact for Migration (2018) — voluntary, non-binding
⚠ Key Distinction to Know
IDP (Internally Displaced Person) — displaced within their own country. Refugee — crosses an international border. Most climate displacement is currently internal, which means UNHCR has even less jurisdiction. Iran should push for definitions that cover BOTH categories.
02 — Iran's Position
Iran: Country Profile & Official Stance
Know your country inside-out. This is what you speak from.
Iran's Core Argument in One Paragraph

Iran is itself a victim of climate change and environmental degradation — facing severe desertification, water scarcity, and internal displacement — while being disproportionately harmed by Western sanctions that cripple its capacity to adapt. Iran argues that the international community must establish legally binding accountability frameworks that hold historically high-emission states (US, EU, UK) responsible for climate damage, provide financial and technical support to vulnerable nations, and formally recognize climate-displaced persons in international law — without allowing powerful states to weaponize climate conditionality against developing nations.

Iran's Climate Situation — Domestic Facts
  • Desertification: Over 90 million hectares — 55% of Iran's total land area — are affected by desertification
  • Lake Urmia: Once the 6th largest saltwater lake on Earth; has shrunk by ~90% since the 1970s — directly displacing communities
  • Dust storms: Khuzestan, Sistan-Baluchestan and western provinces hit by severe sandstorms annually, forcing seasonal displacement
  • Water crisis: Iran is one of the world's most water-stressed nations; 97 of 609 plains have reached critical groundwater depletion
  • Internal climate migrants: Estimated hundreds of thousands displaced from rural areas to cities due to drought and land degradation
  • Heat extremes: Persian Gulf temperatures recorded "wet-bulb" events in 2015 that were physiologically unsurvivable outdoors
Iran's Political & Diplomatic Positioning
  • G77 + China bloc: Iran consistently votes with the Global South on climate finance and historical responsibility
  • Anti-Western conditionality: Iran firmly opposes making climate aid conditional on political compliance — this is seen as imperialism
  • Sanctions impact: US sanctions prevent Iran from accessing green technology, IMF climate funds, and clean energy imports — Iran calls this an environmental human rights violation
  • MENA solidarity: Strong alignment with Algeria, Egypt, Sudan, Morocco on desertification and water rights as human rights
  • Palestine framing: Iran often links environmental dispossession to political dispossession — Gaza's environmental collapse is cited as deliberate ecocide
  • Non-interference principle: Iran opposes international monitoring frameworks that could be used to surveil or intervene in sovereign states

Motion 1 — International Accountability Framework

What it proposes: Establish a binding international framework that holds states and actors accountable for protecting human rights of climate-displaced people.


Iran's position: STRONGLY SUPPORTS — but with conditions. Iran wants accountability directed at historically high-emission Global North states. Iran will push to ensure the framework does not allow powerful states to use "accountability" as a pretext to interfere in developing nations' internal affairs. Iran wants the framework to include provisions against sanctions as climate barriers.

Iran Should Push For
  • Framework based on historical emissions responsibility — polluter pays principle
  • A Climate Justice Tribunal or special rapporteur with developing-nation representation
  • Explicit prohibition on unilateral sanctions that impair climate adaptation capacity
  • Technology transfer obligations from Global North to South
  • Accountability for non-state actors (fossil fuel corporations)
Iran Should Oppose
  • Frameworks that allow interventionist monitoring of developing states
  • Conditionality — tying aid/protection to political behavior
  • Any language that shifts responsibility to developing nations
  • USA/EU-led frameworks without adequate developing-nation voting power
90-Second Speech — Motion 1

Honorable Chair, distinguished delegates —


The Islamic Republic of Iran rises in strong support of establishing an international accountability framework — for the simple reason that accountability without consequence is merely a suggestion.


Climate displacement is not an act of nature. It is the direct consequence of over a century of unchecked industrialization by a handful of wealthy nations. Iran, a country that has contributed less than one percent of cumulative global emissions, now watches as Lake Urmia — once the sixth-largest saltwater lake on Earth — has shrunk by ninety percent. As our western provinces are buried under dust storms. As hundreds of thousands of our own citizens are internally displaced by drought and desertification.


We support this framework — but we demand it be built on the principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities. Accountability must flow from where responsibility lies — from those who built their prosperity on carbon. Furthermore, this Council must recognize that unilateral economic sanctions imposed on developing nations are themselves a human rights violation — they strip states of the very tools needed to protect their climate-vulnerable populations.


Iran calls for a framework that names the polluters, compels the wealthy, and protects the displaced — not one that burdens the already burdened. Thank you.

~90 seconds Tone: Assertive, accusatory toward Global North, data-grounded

90-Second Speech — Motion 3

Honorable Chair, distinguished delegates —


I speak today not from theory, but from the lived reality of my nation. Lake Urmia — once a jewel of the Middle East, the sixth-largest saltwater lake in the world — is now a salt flat. Six million Iranians who depended on that ecosystem breathe salt dust. Their farmlands are gone. Their livelihoods are gone. They are displaced — not by war, not by persecution — but by desertification. And yet they have no legal status. No protection. No recognition.


This is the reality across the Middle East, North Africa, and the African continent. The Sahara expands southward. The Arabian Peninsula becomes less inhabitable. The Sistan Basin loses its water to the vanishing Hamoun Lake. Entire communities are forced to move — and the world calls it migration, not displacement. It calls them migrants, not refugees.


The Islamic Republic of Iran calls on this Council to establish a regional emergency protocol specifically addressing desertification-driven displacement. We call for an expanded Great Green Wall initiative with binding funding commitments. And we demand that the international community recognize that desertification in the MENA region is not a regional problem — it is a global failure of accountability.


The desert is advancing. Our people cannot wait. Thank you.

~90 seconds Tone: Passionate, first-person national experience, urgent, coalition-building
Motion 4 — Strengthening Legal Frameworks for Climate-Displaced Persons

What it proposes: Review and strengthen existing international law to better define and protect "climate-displaced persons," including the undocumented and stateless.


Iran's position: SUPPORTS strongly — but is cautious about how definitions and monitoring mechanisms are structured. Iran wants legal frameworks that protect displaced people AND preserve state sovereignty. Iran will advocate for an expanded definition that includes internally displaced persons and stateless individuals, while opposing any framework that places conditions on receiving states' behavior.

Iran Should Push For
  • A new Protocol to the 1951 Refugee Convention covering climate displacement
  • Recognition of stateless climate-displaced persons as a distinct category deserving automatic protection
  • Closing the loophole for undocumented climate-displaced persons — documentation should not be a prerequisite for human rights
  • Non-refoulement obligations extended to climate displacement
  • Binding financial commitments from high-emission states to receiving countries
Iran Should Oppose / Be Cautious Of
  • Any mechanism allowing international bodies to override sovereign decisions on displacement
  • Western states using expanded refugee definitions to extract political concessions from developing nations hosting displaced persons
  • Definitions that could be retroactively used against Iran regarding its own treatment of Afghan refugees (Iran hosts 3–4M Afghan refugees — largest in region)
  • "Statelessness" frameworks that don't account for the Palestinian stateless situation
💡 Iran's Ace Card — Afghan Refugees
Iran hosts between 3–4 million Afghan refugees — one of the largest refugee-hosting nations in the world, with limited international support and under heavy sanctions. Use this to demonstrate that Iran already bears a massive refugee burden and deserves financial and legal support, not lectures, from the Global North.
90-Second Speech — Motion 4

Honorable Chair —


The Islamic Republic of Iran currently hosts between three and four million Afghan refugees — one of the largest refugee-hosting populations on Earth. We do this with limited resources, under the crushing weight of unilateral sanctions, and with minimal international assistance. We do not lecture the world about burden-sharing. We demonstrate it.


And yet the very people we shelter — including those displaced by Afghanistan's climate collapse, its droughts, its floods — have no formal legal status under international law as climate refugees. They exist in legal limbo. The 1951 Refugee Convention was written for a different world. That world no longer exists.


Iran calls for a substantive reform of international legal frameworks: a new protocol that explicitly defines and protects climate-displaced persons; that guarantees rights to the stateless and undocumented; and that closes every loophole that allows the wealthy world to ignore those who arrive at their borders having lost everything to a crisis they did not create.


But let us be clear: any new legal framework must be built on the principle of sovereign equality. It must not become a tool for powerful states to impose conditionality or intervene in the domestic affairs of receiving nations. Legal protection, yes. Political leverage, never. Thank you.

~90 seconds Tone: Authority from experience (Afghan refugees), reform-focused, sovereignty-protective

07 — Global Context
The Bigger Picture You Need to Know
Facts, frameworks, and key arguments to drop in debate for maximum impact.
Key International Frameworks to Reference
  • 1951 Refugee Convention + 1967 Protocol: Defines refugee as fleeing persecution. Does NOT include climate. Iran wants this extended.
  • Paris Agreement (2015): Acknowledges climate displacement in preamble but gives no binding protections
  • Sendai Framework (2015–2030): Disaster risk reduction, non-binding, no refugee status
  • Global Compact on Refugees (2018): Voluntary, includes climate language, no enforcement
  • Global Compact for Safe Migration (2018): Voluntary, mentions climate migrants
  • Loss and Damage Fund (COP28, 2023): Exists, severely underfunded, no binding contribution rules
  • Kampala Convention (2009): Africa-only, binding IDPs protection — model for global framework
  • Nansen Initiative (2015): Non-binding, addresses cross-border disaster displacement
Devastating Statistics for Debate
  • World Bank: 216 million internal climate migrants by 2050 without action
  • UNHCR: 21.5 million people displaced annually by weather hazards
  • The richest 1% of humanity emit as much carbon as the poorest 50% combined
  • G7 nations responsible for 50% of all historical CO₂ emissions since industrialization
  • Iran's Lake Urmia: lost 90% of its surface area; 6 million people affected
  • Pakistan: 2022 floods displaced 33 million people — climate attribution confirmed
  • Bangladesh: 1 meter of sea-level rise would displace 17 million people
  • Africa: responsible for less than 4% of global emissions but faces most severe climate impacts
  • Iran hosts 3–4 million Afghan refugees — #1 in the world relative to GDP
Iran's Master Narrative — Weave This Through Everything

Iran is a country that is already living the climate displacement crisis — Lake Urmia has nearly vanished, our western provinces are buried in dust, our farmers abandon land that no longer yields. We host millions of the world's refugees while under crippling sanctions that prevent us from accessing the very technologies and funds needed to protect our own people. We are asked to comply with frameworks designed by the nations most responsible for causing this crisis, to be monitored by institutions they dominate, and to implement solutions we can barely afford. The June 2025 "12-Day War" — What Happened

In June 2025, Israel — with US support — launched strikes on Iran's nuclear facilities at Fordow, Isfahan, and Natanz, and assassinated several senior IRGC commanders. Iran retaliated by striking a US military base in Qatar. The conflict lasted approximately 12 days. Iran suffered significant damage to its nuclear infrastructure and exposed serious defensive vulnerabilities. Iran's official position: this was an illegal act of aggression against a sovereign state in violation of the UN Charter.


In committee: If asked about this — "Iran condemns the illegal military aggression committed against our sovereign territory in June 2025. It is precisely this kind of unilateral force that exposes why developing nations cannot trust accountability frameworks dominated by the same powers that bomb them."

Iran's Oil Situation — Right Now
  • Production: Iran holds the world's third-largest proven oil reserves. Despite sanctions, Iran exported ~1.38 million barrels/day of crude to China in 2025 — a drop of only 7% from 2024
  • China as sole buyer: China now accounts for ~90%+ of Iranian crude purchases. After Syria's collapse in late 2024, China became Iran's only crude oil buyer
  • "Shadow Fleet": Iran uses hundreds of non-sanctioned tankers with disabled tracking systems, ship-to-ship transfers near Malaysia and Gulf of Oman, and front companies to evade sanctions — an elaborate covert supply chain
  • Trump's "Maximum Pressure 2.0": Since February 2025, the Trump administration reimposed maximum pressure sanctions aimed at driving Iran's oil exports to zero. New sanctions announced weekly through April 2026 targeting tankers, refineries, and Chinese "teapot" refineries buying Iranian oil
  • Revenue: Total energy export value estimated at ~$60 billion in 2025 — despite sanctions, largely because of the shadow fleet and Chinese demand
  • Infrastructure decay: The bigger long-term threat to Iran's oil sector is not sanctions per se but chronic underinvestment, aging oil fields, and inability to access modern drilling technology — all caused by decades of sanctions
  • Strait of Hormuz: Iran has repeatedly threatened to close the Strait — through which ~20% of global oil transits. This remains Iran's most powerful economic leverage card
Iran's Economy — The Crisis in Numbers
  • Inflation: Exceeded 42–50% in late 2025. Food inflation hit 72% year-on-year. Medical goods up 50%
  • Rial collapse: Before 1979 revolution: ~70 rials to $1. At 2015 JCPOA: ~32,000 rials/$1. January 2026: 1.5 million rials per $1 — a catastrophic depreciation
  • GDP: Oil revenues represent ~25% of Iran's GDP; sanctions on this sector have compounding cascading effects on all other sectors
  • China deal not delivering: Iran's 25-year, $400 billion strategic cooperation agreement with China (covering energy, telecom, transport) has not stabilized the economy
  • Russia partnership: Iran-Russia strategic partnership signed early 2025 — also not producing tangible economic relief
  • US Treasury admitted: In February 2026, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent acknowledged that US sanctions contributed directly to Iran's currency crisis
  • Government response: New Central Bank governor appointed (Abdolnasser Hemmati); government ended preferential exchange rates for most imports; issued largest-ever denomination banknote (10 million rials)
The 2025–2026 Protests — What You Must Know

Beginning December 28, 2025, Iran experienced its largest nationwide protests since the 1979 Islamic Revolution, spreading to all 31 provinces and over 200 cities. Unlike previous movements (Green Movement 2009, Women Life Freedom 2022), these protests cut across all classes: workers, teachers, students, bazaar merchants, pensioners, ethnic minorities.


Trigger: The rial's collapse, 42%+ inflation, food prices up 72%, and the government ending subsidized exchange rates for importers. Shopkeepers in Tehran's Grand Bazaar walked out on January 6, 2026 — a historically significant act.


Government response: Security forces used live fire; internet shut down from January 8; hundreds killed (some estimates 500+), thousands arrested. The regime blamed the US and Israel for instigating protests.


Nuclear context: Protests came in the wake of the June 2025 war with Israel, which exposed Iran's military vulnerabilities and shattered a brief post-war "national unity" moment.


In committee — Iran's official line: "The social and economic difficulties faced by our people are the direct result of illegal unilateral sanctions imposed by Western powers over decades. The international community's failure to recognize the human rights dimension of economic warfare against civilian populations is precisely why we need binding frameworks — not just for climate displacement, but for all forms of externally-imposed suffering."

The Islamic Republic of Iran comes to this committee not as a bystander, not as an observer, but as a nation that is already living the reality that this agenda describes.

10 — Paperwork
Working Paper, Draft Resolution & Amendments
Everything you need to write, co-sponsor, and defend committee paperwork as Iran.
💡 Quick Explainer: WP → DR → Resolution

A Working Paper (WP) is an informal draft — no formal formatting needed, used to negotiate ideas. A Draft Resolution (DR) is the formal, formatted version that gets voted on. DRs have three parts: Preamble (context, existing frameworks, recognizing problems) using preambulatory phrases, and Operative Clauses (what the committee actually decides to do) using operative phrases. Iran should aim to be a main sponsor of a DR, not just a signatory.

Iran's Ideal Working Paper — Key Clauses to Push For

These are the substantive ideas Iran should advocate for in any WP negotiation. Use these to shape the text before it becomes a DR.

Preamble — Iran's Priority Language
  • Recognizing that climate displacement disproportionately affects developing nations and communities that have contributed least to global greenhouse gas emissions
  • Affirming the principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDRRC) as established in the UNFCCC
  • Deeply concerned that existing international legal instruments, including the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol, do not provide adequate protection for persons displaced by climate change and environmental degradation
  • Acknowledging that unilateral coercive economic measures imposed by some states against others impair the capacity of affected nations to adapt to climate change and protect their climate-vulnerable populations
  • Recalling that the right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment was recognized as a human right by the United Nations General Assembly in resolution 76/300 (2022)
  • Noting with concern the accelerating desertification of the Middle East, North Africa, and Sub-Saharan African regions and its documented contribution to population displacement and violations of economic and social rights
  • Acknowledging that women and children face heightened vulnerability in climate displacement contexts and require dedicated protection mechanisms
  • Recognizing that stateless persons and undocumented individuals displaced by climate change exist in a legal protection vacuum requiring urgent remediation
Operative Clauses — Iran's Priority Actions
  • 1. Calls upon all member states to immediately extend non-refoulement protections to persons displaced across international borders by climate change and environmental degradation, regardless of their documentation status
  • 2. Urges the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) to formally expand its mandate to include climate-displaced persons and to develop operational guidelines for their protection within 18 months
  • 3. Recommends the establishment of a Special Rapporteur on the Human Rights of Climate-Displaced Persons, with a mandate to investigate, document, and report on state and corporate accountability for climate displacement
  • 4. Requests the UN Secretary-General to convene an intergovernmental working group to draft a Protocol to the 1951 Refugee Convention explicitly defining and protecting the legal status of climate-displaced persons, including stateless and undocumented individuals
  • 5. Strongly urges historically high-emission states to provide unconditional, accessible, and adequate financing to the Loss and Damage Fund established at COP28, with a minimum annual contribution target proportional to cumulative historical emissions
  • 6. Calls upon all member states to refrain from imposing unilateral coercive economic measures that impair other states' capacities to adapt to climate change or to protect climate-vulnerable populations
  • 7. Encourages the establishment of a Regional Climate Displacement Early Warning System for the Middle East, North Africa, and Sub-Saharan Africa to monitor and respond to desertification-driven displacement
  • 8. Decides to establish gender-responsive protocols ensuring that women and children displaced by climate change receive specialized protection, legal assistance, and access to education and healthcare services, without discrimination
  • 9. Calls for the unconditional transfer of green adaptation technologies from developed to developing nations, recognizing that technology access is a prerequisite for meaningful climate action and displacement prevention
  • 10. Affirms that any international framework for climate-displaced persons must respect the sovereign equality of all member states and may not be used as a pretext for political conditionality or interference in the internal affairs of states
Clauses Iran Should Block / Amend
  • Any operative clause creating an international monitoring body with power to investigate individual member states without their consent — Iran will push to make this advisory-only
  • Language that makes climate finance or refugee protection conditional on recipient states' behavior or compliance with Western political demands
  • Clauses that define the accountability framework's scope in a way that targets present-day emissions equally with historical emissions — Iran is a medium emitter and would be unfairly hit
  • Any language that could be read as endorsing unilateral sanctions as legitimate tools of climate enforcement
  • Mechanisms that would require intrusive border reporting on refugee and displaced person movements — sovereignty concern
Amendment Strategy
  • If a Western-led DR passes that contains conditionality language → friendly amendment to add: "without political conditionality or preconditions inconsistent with the principles of state sovereignty"
  • If a DR on Motion 2 omits the Loss and Damage Fund → operative amendment adding a clause linking sea-level displacement protections to binding Loss and Damage Fund contributions
  • If a DR on Motion 4 creates a monitoring body → amendment specifying it operates "in cooperation with and with the consent of member states"
  • If any DR lacks anti-sanctions language → introduce a preambulatory amendment recognizing that coercive economic measures impair climate adaptation capacity
  • Blocking strategy: If a DR is fundamentally opposed to Iran's positions, organize the G77/China/Russia/Cuba bloc to vote No or abstain — deny it the votes it needs
Preambulatory & Operative Phrases — Quick Reference
Preamble (stronger → weaker)
  • Alarmed by — most urgent
  • Deeply concerned — strong concern
  • Emphasizing / Stressing — key point
  • Affirming / Reaffirming — existing principle
  • Recognizing / Acknowledging — factual
  • Noting / Aware of — weakest, neutral
Operative (stronger → weaker)
  • Demands / Condemns — strongest (rare in UNHRC)
  • Strongly urges / Calls upon — strong request
  • Decides / Establishes — creates binding action
  • Recommends / Requests — formal ask
  • Encourages / Invites — softer, persuasive
  • Notes / Expresses hope — weakest
